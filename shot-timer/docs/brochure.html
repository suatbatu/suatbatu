shot-timer v0.6.0 ESP32-S3 · open source · MIT

A shot timer that knows which bang was yours.

Two microphones, 120 mm apart, cross-correlated to work out which direction every bang came from — so the bay next door stays out of your splits. Plus echo rejection that doesn't cap your rate of fire, an accelerometer for dry fire, and a web interface on any phone.

Roughly $30–45 in parts. The feature that justifies a $330 commercial timer, on a board you can build in an evening.

Build status

Firmware complete and compile-verified. It has never heard a gun.

Every threshold in this document is a reasoned starting point, not a measurement. The detection maths is covered by 40 host-side assertions; the Bluetooth layer has never met a client; the enclosure acoustics — the thing that makes a timer audible through ear protection — are unbuilt. Treat this as a buildable design, not a finished product.

TL;DR

Six lines

  • 1
    It times shots. Random start beep, then every shot recorded to the millisecond — first shot, each split, total, best and worst.
  • 2
    It ignores the next bay. Two microphones measure the arrival-time difference of each bang and reject anything off the shooter's axis.
  • 3
    It ignores echoes without slowing you down. Each shot raises a decaying guard scaled to its own peak, instead of a fixed blanking window that would cap your rate of fire.
  • 4
    It handles dry fire properly. An optional accelerometer treats a trigger break as what it is — a mechanical event — rather than straining to hear it.
  • 5
    It talks to your phone. Its own Wi-Fi access point serves a full web interface; it also speaks the AMG Commander Bluetooth dialect, so PractiScore can read it.
  • 6
    It keeps your data. ~2,000 strings on flash, named drills with trend charts, CSV export, and video overlay.
What the device does

Four questions, in order

A shot timer has one hard job: decide when a bang happened, to a hundredth of a second, in a place full of other bangs. Four questions hide inside that, and each stage only runs on what survived the one before it.

Is it loud enough?

A per-sample envelope follower with instant attack, measured against a noise floor that adapts to the range — and that deliberately stops tracking during a string, so six fast shots can't walk the threshold up and lose the last two.

Is it a shot, or shot 1 off a wall?

Each shot raises a guard 10 dB below its own peak that decays over ~120 ms. A reflection at a third the amplitude is swallowed; a genuine second shot at full level passes straight through.

Is it your shot?

The two microphones are cross-correlated over a 128-sample window around each onset. 120 mm of spacing spans 17 samples across the full 0–90°, so a 25° acceptance window is a real gate. It fails open on any uncertainty — a dropped shot is worse than a neighbour's.

When exactly?

Times come from the I2S sample counter, not from when the CPU got round to looking. That removes the scheduling jitter — hundreds of microseconds, sometimes milliseconds — that a naive timestamp puts straight into your split.

Everything above runs in a dedicated real-time task that never allocates, never touches the network, and never waits on anything. A phone refreshing the live view cannot cost you a split.

On the microphones. A gunshot at the shooter's position is 150–165 dB SPL and no hobbyist MEMS part survives that linearly — an INMP441 clips hard on every centrefire shot. That is fine, and the firmware is built around it: the detector thresholds on an envelope and times the onset, and a clipped transient still has a perfectly well-defined leading edge. It only becomes a limit if you later want to identify shots by their spectral shape, which needs a signal that has one.

MOUNTING

Point the array at the shooter

Not downrange. That puts your muzzle blast at 0° and the neighbouring bays out at 40–80° — exactly the separation the gate needs. Measure the spacing between the ports, not the breakout boards.

ACOUSTICS

Keep the mics off the buzzer

Opposite faces if you can. Detection is muted while the beep sounds, and the further apart the ports are, the shorter that mute window can safely be. Never seal a MEMS mic in — it needs a port.

POWER

Tame the piezo spike

A piezo is capacitive, so the drive edge produces a current spike. If the rail sags enough to reset the board when the buzzer fires — it happens on thin breadboard wiring — add 100 Ω in series and 100 µF across the rail.

Avoid GPIO 0, 19, 20, 43–46 and the 26–32 range on the S3 — they are strapping pins, USB, or wired to internal flash and PSRAM.

Before you build it

What it can't do

Stated here rather than discovered at the range. None of these are bugs; they are the shape of the design.

Shots during the beep aren't recorded

The microphones hear the buzzer centimetres away far better than anything downrange, so detection is muted for the tone plus 40 ms. Every single-array timer makes this trade.

Dry-fire detection needs the sensor on the gun

A timer clipped to your belt will not feel a trigger break in a gun held at arm's length, and no threshold setting changes that. Live-fire recoil does reach a body-worn timer, damped — which is why "both sensors" mode exists.

Beep-to-first-shot has an uncalibrated offset

Split differences are good to well under a millisecond. The constant offset from acoustic travel and microphone group delay is a few milliseconds and has not been measured against a reference timer. A setting exists to trim it once it has.

It is not a match timer

A hobbyist range tool. Don't score a sanctioned match with it — and until the enclosure acoustics are built and measured, expect it to be quieter than anything you can buy.
